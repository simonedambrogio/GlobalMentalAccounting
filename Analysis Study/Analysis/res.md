RESULTS

Our results confirmed that mental accounting is broadly replicable across various countries, although the effects were generally smaller than those reported in the original studies. Additionally, while individual differences such as age, education, income, and financial literacy did not significantly account for variations in mental accounting, a higher GDP per capita was robustly associated with stronger mental accounting effects. Further analysis revealed that mental accounting could manifest differently across decision contexts in which the studies highlight different characteristics of transactions: their social nature, perspective-taking demands, and price-setting roles. This heterogeneity in how mental accounting operates across different financial decisions provides new insights into the psychological mechanisms underlying this phenomenon.

Mental Accounting Widely Replicates

The primary aim of this work was to test the replicability of seven studies on mental accounting using a large sample of participants from around the world. We consider as replication any effect that aligned with the direction reported in the original study and with a 95% statistical credibility. We found an overall replication of 90.5% (133 out of 147; see table 2 in the main text, and figure 1 in Web Appendix), where MrAB and Game achieved a 100% (21/21) replication rate, Drink 95% (20/21), Jacket 81% (17/21), Play 71% (15/21), Gym 90% (19/21), and Plane 95% (20/21). To produce these findings, we calculated effect sizes for each study using a fixed-effect Bayesian regression approach (see figure 1 and table 3 in Web Appendix), setting weakly informative priors for all regression coefficients (normal distribution with mean = 0 and standard deviation = 2.5). This prior choice reflects our assumption that very large effects are unlikely while remaining sufficiently wide to allow the data to dominate the inference.
For the MrAB study, we analyzed participants' responses to scenarios involving gains (MrAB1 and MrAB3) and losses (MrAB2 and MrAB4) separately. According to mental accounting theory, when experiencing multiple monetary events, people should prefer experiencing gains separately (e.g., winning $50 and $25 in two different lotteries rather than winning $75 in one lottery) but prefer experiencing losses together (e.g., receiving one fine of $150 rather than two separate fines of $100 and $50). To quantify these effects, we calculated the proportion of participants showing these predicted response patterns for each country using a Bayesian binomial regression model and compared it against what we would expect by chance. The resulting effect size represents how much more likely participants were to show mental accounting patterns compared to random choice.
For the Drink and Gym studies, which involved continuous responses rather than categorical choices, we used Bayesian linear regression models to quantify the mental accounting effects.
In the Drink study, mental accounting theory predicts that people will find it more acceptable to pay higher prices in upscale contexts compared to modest ones. To quantify it, we used a Bayesian linear regression model predicting the logarithm of prices from the store type (fancy resort hotel vs. run-down grocery store), while accounting for country-specific variations in both baseline prices and the effect of store type.
 Similarly, for the Gym study, the theory predicts that people perceive wasting money differently depending on how payments are framed. To test this, we predicted participants' ratings about wasting money from the payment frame (per-session versus yearly membership), while accounting for country-specific variations in both baseline ratings and the effect of the payment frame.
For the remaining studies (Game, Jacket, Play, and Plane), which involved binary choices, we calculated effect sizes using Bayesian binomial regression models.
In the Game study, mental accounting theory predicts that people set different prices for a sports ticket based on their relationship with the buyer (friend vs. stranger) and the ticket's acquisition context. To quantify this, we modeled the probability that participants would set a price equal to or above market value as a function of buyer type, while accounting for country-specific variations.
In the Jacket study, the theory predicts that people's willingness to make an extra trip to save money depends on the relative rather than absolute price difference. To test this, we modeled the probability of making the trip to another store as a function of the price magnitude (high vs. low), while accounting for country-specific variations.
In the Play study, the theory predicts that people make different decisions about attending an event depending on whether they previously lost the ticket or an equivalent amount of cash. To examine this, we modeled the probability of paying $40 as a function of the loss type (cash vs. ticket), while accounting for country-specific variations.
Finally, for the Plane study, the theory predicts that people's willingness to pay for an upgrade coupon depends on how the coupon was acquired. To test this, we modeled the probability that participants would choose to pay their friend $35 as a function of the coupon type (purchased vs. free), while accounting for country-specific variations.
To complement these unpooled analyses with a method that emphasizes the commonalities across countries, we performed a hierarchical Bayesian meta-analysis to pool data across countries while accounting for country-specific variations. This approach allows us to draw more generalizable conclusions about mental accounting effects while still preserving information about country-level differences. We fitted for each study the Bayesian model to the observed effect size and standard error of each country and found a 100% (7/7 studies) replication rate (see figure 1 and table 2, see Section 7, Web Appendix for effect sizes and standard error computations). Again, we used weakly informative priors for all regression coefficients (normal distribution with mean = 0 and standard deviation = 2.5). For all the studies where we could compare the effect sizes, we found a smaller effect than in the original studies. This finding aligns with other replication efforts (Open Science Collaboration 2015) and suggests that the use of smaller sample sizes in the original studies might have resulted in inflated estimates of effect size.

While our studies utilized a within-subject design for all studies, some of the original studies (Jacket, Play, and Plane) employed a between-subject design. To exclude the possibility that our design choice may have affected the results compared to the original studies, we conducted additional analyses for the Jacket, Play, and Plane studies. We simulated a between-subject design by randomly assigning participants to one condition, effectively treating the data as if it came from a between-subject experiment. The differences in effect sizes between the within and between-subject analyses were not statistically credible (Jacket: 95% CI [-0.10, 0.22]; Play: 95% CI [-0.25, 0.30]; Plane: 95% CI [-0.23, 0.28], see figure 13,  Web Appendix), suggesting that our results are robust to experimental design choices.

GDP per Capita, but Not Financial Literacy nor Demographic Variables, Can Explain Some Inter-Country Variability

Having established the robustness of mental accounting effects across countries, we next investigated what factors might explain the variations in effect sizes between countries shown in figure 1. We first examined whether individual differences such as Age, Education, Personal Income, and Financial Literacy could account for these variations. We converted the log odd-ratios from the Game, Jacket, Play, and Plane studies into the same metric used for the Drink and Gym studies (standardized mean difference, also known as Cohen's d), to combine the mental accounting effects of different studies (Chinn 2000). MrAB was excluded from these analyses since its measure of effect size could not be mapped onto a Cohen’s d. We then ran a hierarchical Bayesian analysis in which Financial Literacy, Education, Age, and Income were modeled as fixed-effects and random slopes, and the variable study was modeled as a random intercept and set weakly informative priors for all regression coefficients (normal distribution with mean = 0 and standard deviation = 2.5). We did not find any credible effects for any of the four predictors (see table 3). 
To exclude the possibility that the lack of significant effects was due to the inclusion of all demographic variables in a single analysis, we considered each predictor separately. We fitted individual models for Financial Literacy, Education, Age, and Income, each including both fixed effects and random slopes by study. These separate analyses confirmed our initial findings, showing no credible effects for any of the individual difference variables when examined independently. These results indicate that mental accounting does not depend on the individual difference factors that we considered, even when potential correlations between predictors are eliminated from the analysis (table 7 in the Web Appendix).

To further validate this conclusion, we conducted more granular analyses where we considered each participant, country, and study separately. Also, in this case we fitted individual models for Financial Literacy, Education, Age, and Personal Income, each including both fixed effects and random slopes by country as well as a model with all the predictors. Both approaches confirmed the absence of a robust association between the four demographic variables and mental accounting (figure 2 in Web Appendix).
Next, we examined whether the broader economic context of a country might influence the extent of mental accounting by focusing on the relationship between GDP per capita and mental accounting. We found a positive association between GDP per capita and mental accounting up to approximately $50,000 (slope = 2.5, 95% CIs = [0.69 – 4.68]) and a plateau effect for higher values (see figure 2). To obtain this result, first, we tested for the presence of both a linear and a non-linear relationship, fitting a Bayesian hierarchical linear regression to test the first hypothesis and a Bayesian hierarchical logistic growth model to test the second. We then compared the predictive performance of the two models using leave-one-out cross-validation and found that the non-linear model outperformed the linear model. The difference in expected log pointwise predictive density (ELPD) was -12.7 (standard error = 4.5), indicating a substantially higher predictive accuracy for the non-linear model. These findings suggest that countries with higher GDP per capita demonstrate stronger mental accounting, but this effect levels off at higher economic thresholds. 
To investigate whether this GDP effect might be capturing other country-level differences, we considered the Denomination value of the currency and Democracy index. We reasoned that the Denomination value of the currency could influence the mental accounting effects and the Democracy index could capture other country-level differences that are correlated with GDP per capita. We fitted a nonlinear model including GDP per capita, currency denomination, and the Democracy index, and found no evidence of a Denomination value effect (β = -0.05, 95% CIs = [-0.18, 0.07]) and Democracy index effect (β = -0.07, 95% CIs = [-0.16, 0.04]), while we still found a robust GDP effect (growth rate = 2.56, 95% CIs = [0.31, 5.14]).

Heterogeneity in Scenarios

While our results confirm the broad replicability of mental accounting effects, the variation in replication rates across scenarios (from 71% to 100%) suggests that mental accounting might manifest differently across different decision contexts. Understanding this heterogeneity could reveal distinct psychological mechanisms underlying different types of mental accounting decisions. To explore how our seven scenarios relate to each other, we conducted a multidimensional scaling (MDS) analysis. This analysis helps visualize the underlying structure of our scenarios by representing their relationships in a low-dimensional space, where distances between scenarios reflect their similarities in how participants respond to them across countries.
The MDS revealed three dimensions that together explain 77.8% of the variance in scenario relationships. We propose the following interpretations for these dimensions, while acknowledging that alternative interpretations might be possible. The first dimension (34.3% of variance) appears to distinguish between scenarios that involve interaction with others (e.g., Drink asking about "a friend offering to buy beer") and those that present purely solitary decisions (e.g., Jacket asking about comparing prices across stores). The second dimension (27.7% of variance) seems to separate scenarios based on whose perspective is being considered: whether participants evaluate others' experiences (e.g., MrAB asking "Who is happier?") versus making decisions for themselves (e.g., Jacket asking "Would you make the trip?"). The third dimension (16.6% of variance) differentiates scenarios by the participant's role in price determination: from actively setting prices (e.g., Game asking "What price do you ask for the ticket?"), to evaluating fixed amounts (e.g., Gym's fixed membership fee), to deciding whether to accept predetermined prices (e.g., Play asking "Would you pay $40 for another ticket?")
